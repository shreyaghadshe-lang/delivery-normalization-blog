One tap, five kinds of data

Every order on Swiggy or Zomato touches information about a customer, a restaurant, menu items, a payment and a delivery partner. Millions of orders a day means millions of chances for that information to drift out of sync.

Normalization is the discipline that prevents this. It organizes data into well-structured tables so that every fact is stored in exactly one place, and everything else points to it.

Three anomalies that follow

Insertion anomaly

A new restaurant cannot be added until someone orders from it, because the table only stores restaurants inside order rows.

Update anomaly

Changing a price or phone number means editing every matching row. Miss one and the app shows two prices for the same dish.

Deletion anomaly

Delete Meera’s only order from Spice Route and the restaurant’s Paneer Tikka entry disappears with it.

Step 1: find the dependencies

A functional dependency says that knowing one attribute tells you another. These are the ones hiding in our order data:

  • customer_id → name, phone
  • restaurant_id → name, city
  • item_id → item_name, price, restaurant_id
  • order_id → customer_id, order_date, status
  • (order_id, item_id) → quantity

Step 2: apply the normal forms

1NF: one value per cell

An order that stores “Pizza, Coke, Fries” in one cell breaks the rule. Each item gets its own row, and every row is identifiable.

2NF: no partial dependencies

The key of the order-item rows is (order_id, item_id), yet item price depends only on item_id and customer details only on order_id. Move each fact to the table of the thing it describes.

3NF: no transitive dependencies

If an order points to a restaurant and the restaurant determines its city, the city does not belong in the Order table. Every attribute should depend on the key, the whole key and nothing but the key.

BCNF: the final tightening

Every determinant must be a candidate key. In this schema, 3NF already satisfies it, which is a good sign the design is sound.

The payoff. A phone number is edited in one row. A restaurant can be listed before its first order. Deleting an order leaves menus and customers untouched. Refunds point to exactly one payment.

What it means for each kind of data

  • Customer: one profile reused across every order and address.
  • Restaurant: menus and prices updated once and correct everywhere.
  • Order: clean links between who ordered, what, where and who delivered.
  • Payment: each transaction recorded once, which keeps reports and refunds reliable.

The honest trade-off

More tables means more joins, and joins cost time when millions of people open the app at dinner time. Large platforms therefore denormalize on purpose, for example by caching a restaurant’s average rating or storing an order total. The sensible path is to start normalized, then break the rules deliberately and only where measurements show a need.

Conclusion

When your biryani arrives at the right door with the right bill, a well-normalized database did part of that work. It is invisible in the app, but it is what keeps customer, restaurant, order and payment data accurate, consistent and ready to scale.
